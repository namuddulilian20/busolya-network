Farm accountancy · Pre-launch edition

Why a farm without books is a farm without a price

A manager can tell you the harvest. Very few can tell you what it cost. Until someone can, the farm has no price, no credit record and no way to prove that the last season was better than the one before.

Read the three records that matter

1. The daily input and labour book. What went into the soil and who was paid to put it there. Seed, cuttings, feed, drugs, fuel, casual labour. One line per day, written the same day. The value is not the neatness — it is that the figure exists at all when a buyer or a lender asks.

2. The stock book. Livestock counts, bags in store, produce drying, tools. A farm loses more to unrecorded stock than to bad weather: a bag that leaves without a line is a sale that never happened.

3. The season summary. At the end of the season, one page: total cost, total sale, what was consumed at home, and what is still owed. That page is the farm's curriculum vitae. Two seasons of it and a SACCO can price a loan instead of guessing.

Rule for Farm Aid managers: books are part of the job description, not an extra. Proceeds are shared against recorded numbers, so an unwritten season is an unsettled season.

Land & tenure · Pre-launch edition

Bataka, mailo and kibanja in plain language

Most disputes that reach a farm gate are not about farming. They are about who holds what, and who was never asked. Getting the words right is cheaper than getting a lawyer.

Read the working definitions

Omutaka / Bataka. A land owner who pays allegiance to the Kingdom of Buganda: affiliation, servitude where custom requires it, and the land dues owed to the Kingdom. In everyday use, the clan heads who hold land under the Kingdom.

Mailo. Registered land held in perpetuity, measured in square miles. It can be owned, sold and inherited, and it often carries occupants who are not the registered owner.

Kibanja and busuulu. A kibanja is the occupier's holding on someone else's registered land. Busuulu is the annual dues the occupier pays for it. A kibanja is a relationship as much as a plot, and it survives a change of registered owner only if the relationship is documented.

Why this matters to Farm Aid. Farm Aid works dormant land. Before a single cutting goes in, the agreement must name the holder, the occupier, the term, the shares and the exit. Verbal permission from a relative is not a title.

Culture · Pre-launch edition

Ekika to Enju: how a clan is layered

A clan is not a list of names. It is a stack of institutions, each one small enough to convene and large enough to matter — which is exactly why a farmers' network can be built on it.

Read the six layers

Ekika — the clan itself. Essiga — the hearth-stone, one of the three stones under the pot: a sub-clan. Omutuba — the tree whose bark makes cloth: a named branch of descent. Olunyiriri — literally a line: the lineage that marks who follows whom. Oluggya — the front compound where food is dried and people gather. Enju — the homestead, the great-grandfather's line, counted to about the fifth generation.

Each layer has its head and a Katikkiro, the premier who represents that head and can be removed by them. Other offices are settled by proposal and consensus. Land, custom and duty to the Kingdom run through the whole stack.

This is a full expo subject, taught at the expo rather than summarised here: what allegiance carries, who convenes which layer, and how a supplier or a guarantor is introduced inside it.

Market access · Pre-launch edition

Your best buyer is already in the log

The expo produces hundreds of visitor answers. Almost all of them describe something someone wanted to buy. The only reason a farm leaves without a sale is that nobody matched the two records.

Read how a match is made

Every visitor answer becomes a demand entry: product, quantity, district, budget, timing, credit need. Every exhibitor becomes a supply entry: category, capacity, price band, coverage.

A match needs three things to hold: the product, the timing, and someone who stands behind the payment. That third one is why the credit desk records a creditor or guarantor against the lead rather than against a handshake.

Repeat matches are the point. The first order proves the supplier; the second one is priced better because the record already exists.
